# Saturday shopping
Saturday morning is market morning. I leave around nine, before the crowds arrive, with two cloth bags and a rough idea of what I need. The market sits in the square behind the church, and by ten it's humming — vendors calling out prices, kids weaving between the stalls, everyone comparing tomatoes.

I start at the fruit stand at the far end because the best peaches go early. The seller knows me by now; she'll hold back a bag if I ask, and she always slips an extra apple in "for the walk home." From there I work my way through: vegetables, eggs, bread from the bakery stall, and flowers if it's been a long week.

Shopping here is slower than the supermarket, and that's the point. You talk. You taste. You learn that the plums are better this week than last, that the cheese maker's cows came back to the summer pasture, that frost is coming early this year. None of that fits on a barcode.

After the market I'll stop at the bookshop two streets over, just to look. I almost always leave with something. The owner keeps a shelf of secondhand novels by the window, and there's a particular armchair by the radiator that has cost me many afternoons and a fair amount of money.

By noon my bags are heavy and my feet are tired, in a satisfying way. I walk home the long way, past the school and the little park where the pigeons gather. Lunch is whatever looked best at the market — bread, cheese, fruit — eaten at the kitchen table with the newspaper I haven't opened yet.

Some people find markets chaotic, but I find them honest. The food was in the ground or on a tree a day or two ago, and the person selling it grew it or chose it. In a week full of screens and schedules, an hour of choosing my own apples feels like being part of something real.
