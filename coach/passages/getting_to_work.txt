# Getting to work
On weekdays my alarm goes off at six thirty, and I'm usually out of the door by seven. I walk to the station, whatever the weather, because it wakes me up better than coffee does. The train comes every ten minutes or so, and if I catch the earlier one, I can get a seat and read a few pages of my book.

The journey takes about half an hour. Most people keep to themselves — some sleep, some scroll through their phones, and a few of us read. Every now and then the train gets stuck behind a slower one, and you can feel the whole carriage sigh at once. I've learned not to let it bother me; it's a good excuse to finish a chapter.

I get off two stops before the office and walk the rest of the way. There's a bakery on the corner, and the smell of fresh bread drifts all the way down the street. Sometimes I'll pick up a pastry if I'm feeling hungry, but I try not to make a habit of it. The woman who runs the shop always says good morning, even when it's raining.

If the weather's really bad, I take the bus instead. It's slower, but you don't get wet, and I can usually find a place to sit. The bus route goes past the river, and on a clear morning the water looks almost silver. I never get tired of that view, even after all these years.

Going home is the mirror image of the morning, only slower. The trains are busier, everyone's tired, and nobody makes eye contact. I usually put on a podcast and let someone else's voice carry me home. By the time I reach my door, the day has already started to fade.

I know some people hate commuting, but I've made peace with it. It's the one time of day that belongs entirely to me — no messages, no meetings, nothing to fix. Just a seat, a book, and half an hour of quiet between the two halves of my life.
